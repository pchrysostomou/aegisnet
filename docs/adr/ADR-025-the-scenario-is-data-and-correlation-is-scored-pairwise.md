# ADR-025 — The scenario is data, and correlation is scored pairwise

- Status: accepted
- Date: 2026-09-06
- Milestone: 3 (Chunk 17); completes the M3 acceptance criteria begun in
  [ADR-023](ADR-023-correlation-and-incidents.md) and [ADR-024](ADR-024-incident-api-workflow-enforcement-and-analyst-text.md)

## Context

`docs/delivery-plan.md` M3 asks for one thing this project could not yet demonstrate: a
scripted multi-stage scenario — a scan, then auth failures, then beaconing, then a large
upload from one asset — that produces **exactly one incident with four alerts from four
distinct rules and an escalated severity**. And §8 of `docs/evaluation.md` has carried three
empty correlation metrics since Milestone 0.

Both are claims about behaviour, and this repository's standing rule is that a claim without
evidence is not done. The question is what shape the evidence takes.

There is a fourth rule in the way. D-005 compares an hour against the asset's own history and
**abstains** without a baseline, which is correct and is why it stayed silent on the real lab
capture (`docs/evaluation.md` §9). A scenario that skipped it would meet the letter of the
criterion with three rules and quietly drop the one that needs the most from the system
around it.

## Decision

### The scenario is committed data, not a test fixture

`samples/scenarios/multi-stage-01.ndjson` is 303 EVE records generated by
`tools/gen_demo_scenario.py` from one seed, registered in `samples/registry.yml` with a
pinned sha256 and a manifest beside it. It is the same kind of artefact as the benign corpus:
a reviewer can read it, `make demo-scenario` ingests it through the real ingest path, and a
test proves regenerating from the seed reproduces the committed bytes exactly.

A fixture would have been less work and worth less. Data that goes through ingest,
normalisation, the sweep, the baseline job and correlation exercises all of them; a fixture
handed straight to a detector exercises the detector.

**A week of history is part of the scenario.** 168 hourly transfers of four to six MiB from
the compromised host, before anything happens, are what let `recompute_baselines` produce the
baseline D-005 needs. Without them the scenario would be measuring a rule that abstains.

**The scenario is dated inside the evaluation clock's freshness window.** Every committed
corpus is judged against `EVALUATION_CLOCK` (2026-09-05T12:00Z), and the normaliser refuses a
timestamp more than 24 hours in the future or more than ten years in the past (T-1.7). The
history begins seven days before the clock; the afternoon scan ends 3.1 hours after it, which
is inside the 24-hour tolerance — 36 of the 303 records sit after the clock and all are
accepted. The first draft was dated three days after it, and more than half its records were
refused by the very guard this demo exists to exercise. The margin is 21 hours, so a future
scenario that adds a later stage has to check it rather than assume it.

**There are three stories, and two of them share a host.** A second address scans in the same
hour, unrelated to the compromise. And the compromised host itself scans again six hours
later — the same entity, a different story, far outside correlation's one-hour join gap.

That third scenario is the one that makes the measurement mean anything, and it was added
because the first version of this harness did not have it. Ground truth was originally derived
by looking each alert's entity key up in a table keyed by entity. Correlation groups *by
entity key*. So two alerts shared a case exactly when they shared a truth label: a false
positive pair was impossible, a contaminated case was impossible, and precision and
contamination were pinned at 1.00 and 0.00 for every conceivable input. They looked like
measurements and were arithmetic identities.

Ground truth is therefore declared in the manifest as a scenario id, an entity **and a time
window**, and an alert belongs to the scenario whose entity it matches and whose window
contains it. Nothing about the grouping is consulted. Widening the join gap to 24 hours now
folds the morning compromise and the afternoon scan into one case and the numbers move:
precision 0.60, fragmentation 0.67, contamination 0.50. A test asserts exactly that, because a
metric that cannot report a regression is decoration.

### Correlation is scored pairwise

`domain/correlation_eval.py` scores a grouping against known truth by asking, for every pair
of alerts, two questions: did correlation put these two in one case, and should it have.

Pairwise because the two failure modes are opposite and case counting hides both. A rule that
puts everything in one case has perfect recall and terrible precision. A rule that gives every
alert its own case has the opposite. Counting incidents alone would score both as "wrong by
one" and say nothing about *how*.

The three measures §4 asks for come out of that: grouping precision and recall from the pairs,
fragmentation as incidents produced ÷ expected, contamination as the share of incidents
holding alerts from more than one scenario. §4 gained the grouping precision/recall target it
had been missing, so the renderer publishes a target the plan sets rather than one it invents.

A scenario's ground truth names the rules it expects to fire, and each of those gets a stable
id whether or not an alert appeared. An expected alert that never arrives is therefore a pair
the grouping failed to make, counted against recall — so a detector that goes quiet, or an
engine that drops evidence, cannot improve the score by producing less. The published ratios
carry the pair counts they rest on, because `1.00` over six pairs and `1.00` over none are the
same two characters and very different claims.

### The pure harness and the stack demo both run, and they disagree in one known way

`make eval` runs the scenario in-process — no database, no queue — and writes the numbers into
§8 between markers a test pins. `make demo-scenario` runs the same bytes through the whole
stack: seed the assets, ingest, recompute baselines as of the scenario's own hour, sweep,
correlate, list the cases.

They agree on the grouping, and they reach the same severity by different arithmetic. The
stored formula (ADR-018) is
`clamp(floor(base + 0.5·(criticality − 3) + 2·(signal − 0.5) + 0.5), 1, 5)`; a pure run has
neither an asset inventory nor a stored alert, so it uses each rule's base severity. Both
routes put the compromise at 5 and both mark it escalated, which is the property being
claimed — but the §8 block says which numbers produced it rather than letting a reader assume
the stack's.

The reconstruction is checked against the real thing rather than asserted: on the live stack
the baseline job wrote 168 samples, mean 5138022.0, stddev 629145.6, p95 6081740.0, and the
pure harness computes those four numbers exactly. `baselines_from_history` applies every
clause `event_read_store.hourly_outbound_bytes` applies — flow events only, with a
destination, to a non-internal address, from inside the asset's networks, over the complete
hours of the window — because a reconstruction that counted different events would publish
numbers describing a baseline D-005 was never compared against.

`recompute-baselines` gained a `--until` flag for this. The job summarises the complete hours
before *now* by default, which cannot see a committed corpus dated in the past. Replaying
history as of the hour it describes is a real operator need, not a demo affordance.

## Consequences

- Positive: the M3 acceptance criterion is now a test over committed bytes rather than a
  sentence in a plan. On the stack the compromise becomes one case titled "4 rules on
  10.10.0.42: D-001, D-002, D-004, D-005", carrying four alerts at severity 5, escalated,
  beside two separate single-rule cases.
- Positive: §8's correlation metrics are generated, pinned, and reported with their targets, so
  a change to the grouping policy that makes them worse cannot be committed quietly.
- Positive: D-005 is exercised end to end for the first time — the rule the lab could not test
  because an hour of traffic cannot furnish 24 hourly samples.
- Positive: the metrics are honest about the shapes they punish. The unit tests assert what each
  number does when correlation lumps everything together and when it splits everything apart,
  and the scenario test proves a worse grouping scores worse on the committed data.
- Negative: the scenario is synthetic, and its numbers say nothing about real traffic. The
  contamination measure has exactly one opportunity to be non-zero — the two stories that share
  a host — which is enough to catch an over-merging policy and far fewer than a real fleet
  offers. The over-splitting direction is not reachable here at all: the four alerts of the
  compromise overlap in time, so shrinking the join gap does not separate them, and
  fragmentation above 1.0 stays untested by this corpus.
- Negative: a second committed corpus is a second sha256 to keep in step. The registry test
  and the determinism test catch it, at the cost of regenerating and re-pinning whenever the
  generator changes.
- Neutral: the scenario adds a `samples/scenarios/` directory and two assets to the seed set.
  Nothing else moves; there is no schema change and no new revision.
